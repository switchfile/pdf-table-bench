## 1. Introduction

Border closure regimes in the ECOWAS corridor are typically justified as instruments of security: they are said to interdict smuggling networks, disrupt informal cross-border trade that evades customs control, and restore the state's monopoly over legitimate commerce. The policy logic is displacement-based. Closing a formal border crossing is expected to raise the cost of smuggling through that crossing, which is expected to shift informal trade to alternative routes, which is expected to make those alternative routes observable and therefore interdiction targets. Each link in this chain is a claim about a market-displacement effect, and each is testable in principle.

The empirical question is whether the designs used to test displacement can detect it.

The literature on informal cross-border trade in West Africa has established that smuggling networks are adaptive and spatially mobile. Meagher (2010) showed that informal trade networks in the Nigeria-Benin corridor reorganise around enforcement shocks rather than dissolving under them, and Golub (2015) documented how the porosity of the corridor sustains a parallel economy that formal closure regimes displace rather than eliminate.

These findings are consistent with the displacement hypothesis, but they are largely qualitative. The quantitative designs that have been brought to bear on the question are typically before-after comparisons of border crossings, sometimes with a comparison group of unaffected crossings, and sometimes with a computed control constructed from a weighted combination of untreated units.

The before-after-control-impact (BACI) design is the workhorse of this literature, and its operating characteristics are the subject of the present analysis.

The BACI design compares the change in an outcome at treated crossings with the change at control crossings. The estimator is the difference-in-differences interaction, and its identifying assumption is that the treated and control crossings would have followed parallel trends absent the closure. In the ECOWAS corridor, the number of crossings available for analysis is small. A study of the Nigeria-Benin closure might have eight treated crossings and eight controls; a study of the Ghana-Togo corridor might have sixteen.

The question is not whether border closures displace informal trade. The qualitative evidence suggests that they do. The question is whether the designs used to estimate displacement can distinguish a displacement effect of the magnitude the literature hypothesises from sampling noise. If the answer is no, then the quantitative literature on border closure regimes is reporting estimates that are too imprecise to adjudicate between competing accounts of smuggling-network adaptation.

## 2. Evidence and Gaps

The analysis characterises the BACI design under a declared variance structure rather than an estimated one. The between-cluster standard deviation is 6.0, the residual standard deviation is 4.5, and the intraclass correlation is 0.640. These values are not estimated from corridor data; they are declared parameters that define the design space within which the operating characteristics are computed. The intraclass correlation of 0.640 is the quantity that governs the design's efficiency.

When the intraclass correlation is high, the effective sample size is much smaller than the nominal cluster count, and the BACI interaction inherits that reduction.

The intraclass correlation of 0.640 is the single most consequential parameter in the analysis. It implies that roughly two-thirds of the variance in the outcome is between clusters rather than within them. In the corridor setting, this is plausible: crossings differ systematically in traffic volume, enforcement intensity, and the structure of the informal networks that operate through them, and those differences are stable over time. A design that compares a small number of crossings is therefore comparing a small number of highly heterogeneous units, and the precision of the interaction estimate is correspondingly limited.

Power was estimated across 2000 design cells defined by cluster counts of 8, 16, 24 and 32 and effect sizes of 3, 5 and 8 percentage points, with 2000 replications per cell. The cluster counts span the range that is realistic for corridor research: eight crossings is a small study of a single bilateral corridor, sixteen is a multi-corridor study, twenty-four is a regional study, and thirty-two is at the upper end of what the corridor's crossing density can support. The effect sizes span the range that the policy literature hypothesises: a 3-percentage-point displacement is a modest reallocation of informal trade, a 5-percentage-point displacement is a substantial one, and an 8percentage-point displacement is a large one.

The replications per cell are 2000, which is sufficient to estimate power to within roughly one percentage point at conventional confidence levels. The design grid is therefore dense enough to characterise the power surface without being so dense that the computation becomes prohibitive.

The results reported below are the operating characteristics of the BACI interaction under this declared variance structure, and they should be read as properties of the design rather than as estimates of any particular closure regime's effect.

## 3. Power to detect displacement across the design grid

Figure 1 shows estimated power against effect size for each cluster count. The horizontal axis is the effect in percentage points, and the vertical axis is estimated power. The curves are ordered by cluster count: the eightcluster curve lies below the sixteen-cluster curve, which lies below the twenty-four-cluster curve, which lies below the thirty-two-cluster curve. At an effect size of 3 percentage points, none of the four curves reaches the conventional 80 per cent threshold.

At an effect size of 5 percentage points, the thirty-two-cluster curve approaches the threshold, but the eight-cluster and sixteen-cluster curves remain well below it. At an effect size of 8 percentage points, the thirty-two-cluster and twenty-four-cluster curves exceed the threshold, while the eight-cluster curve remains below it.

The pattern is the one that the intraclass correlation of 0.640 predicts. Because two-thirds of the variance is between clusters, adding clusters adds information slowly. The eight-cluster design has an effective sample size that is a small fraction of its nominal size, and its power to detect a 5-percentage-point effect is correspondingly low. The thirty-two-cluster design performs better, but even it does not reach 80 per cent power at a 3-percentagepoint effect.

The power curves therefore establish a clear result: the BACI design's ability to detect displacement is governed by the cluster count, and the cluster counts typical of corridor research are at the low end of the range where detection is feasible.

Figure 2 shows the minimum detectable effect at 80 per cent power against the number of clusters. The horizontal axis is the cluster count, and the vertical axis is the minimum detectable effect in percentage points. The curve is downward-sloping: as the cluster count increases, the minimum detectable effect falls. At eight clusters, the minimum detectable effect is large; at sixteen clusters, it is smaller; at twenty-four clusters, it is smaller still; and at thirty-two clusters, it approaches the 5-percentage-point range.

The curve is steep at the low end and flattens at the high end, which is the signature of a design whose precision is limited by between-cluster heterogeneity.

The minimum detectable effect curve is the more policy-relevant of the two power summaries. A study of a border closure regime that has eight clusters cannot detect a displacement effect smaller than the value the curve reports at eight clusters, regardless of how many replications the analyst runs or how carefully the outcome is measured. The design's resolution is a property of the cluster count and the intraclass correlation, and it is not something that additional within-cluster observations can repair. This is the sense in which the BACI design imposes a binding constraint on the study of border closure regimes: the constraint is structural, and it operates through the cluster count.

Figure 3 shows power against clusters. The horizontal axis is the cluster count, and the vertical axis is estimated power. The scatter of points traces the same downward-sloping relationship as Figure 2, viewed from the other direction: as the cluster count increases, power increases. The scatter is not a smooth curve because the power estimates are computed at the three effect sizes in the design grid, and the points therefore cluster at the effectsize-specific power levels.

The figure makes visible the fact that the power surface is not a single curve but a family of curves, one for each effect size, and that the cluster count required for adequate power depends on which effect size the analyst is trying to detect.

The cluster count required to detect a 5-percentage-point effect at 80 per cent power is 54. This is the central numerical result of the analysis. The design grid's largest cluster count is 32, and the power curve at 32 clusters does not reach 80 per cent at a 5-percentage-point effect. The required cluster count of 54 is therefore outside the design grid, which means that the grid's upper end is still short of the threshold for the effect size that the policy literature treats as substantial.

A corridor study with 32 clusters is better powered than one with 8, but it is not adequately powered to detect a 5percentage-point displacement effect.

## 4. Estimation precision and the interpretation of corridor evidence

Figure 4 shows the estimate with its 95 per cent confidence interval by effect size. The horizontal axis is the estimate, and the vertical axis is the effect size in percentage points. The figure displays three intervals, one for each effect size in the design grid, and the intervals widen as the effect size falls. The 8-percentage-point interval is the narrowest, the 5-percentage-point interval is wider, and the 3-percentage-point interval is widest.

The widening reflects the fact that smaller effects are harder to distinguish from zero, and the confidence interval is the natural summary of that difficulty.

The confidence intervals in Figure 4 are the quantities that a reader of a corridor study would actually see. A study that reports a 5-percentage-point displacement estimate with a confidence interval that spans zero has not established displacement, even if the point estimate is in the hypothesised direction. The BACI design's operating characteristics determine how often that interval will span zero when a true effect is present, and the power curves in Figure 1 are the complement of that probability. The confidence-interval perspective and the power perspective are therefore two views of the same design property, and both point to the same conclusion: the cluster count is the binding constraint.

Figure 5 shows a kernel density estimate of the estimated power. The horizontal axis is estimated power, and the vertical axis is density. The density is bimodal, with one mode at low power and another at high power. The lowpower mode corresponds to the design cells with small cluster counts and small effect sizes, and the high-power mode corresponds to the cells with large cluster counts and large effect sizes.

The bimodality is a feature of the design grid rather than of any particular corridor, but it has an interpretive consequence: a study that falls in the low-power mode is not simply a less precise version of a study that falls in the high-power mode. It is a study that is unlikely to detect the effect it is looking for, and its null results should be read accordingly.

The density in Figure 5 also shows that the power estimates are spread across the full range from near zero to near one. There is no concentration of power estimates at the conventional 80 per cent threshold, which means that the design grid does not cluster around the threshold in a way that would make the threshold a natural summary of the grid. The grid instead spans the range from clearly underpowered to clearly overpowered, and the analyst's task is to locate a particular corridor study within that range. The cluster count and the effect size are the two coordinates that locate the study, and the power surface is the map.

Figure 6 shows a receiver operating characteristic curve. The horizontal axis is the false positive rate, and the vertical axis is the true positive rate. The curve lies above the 45-degree diagonal, which indicates that the BACI interaction discriminates between displacement and no displacement better than chance. The area under the curve is a summary of that discrimination, and the curve's shape shows that the discrimination is better at high false positive rates than at low ones.

A study that insists on a stringent false positive rate will therefore have a low true positive rate, which is another way of saying that the design's power is limited.

The ROC curve is the natural summary of the design's operating characteristics when the analyst is uncertain about the effect size. The power curves in Figure 1 are conditional on a particular effect size, but the ROC curve integrates over the effect-size distribution and reports the trade-off between false positives and true positives. The curve's position above the diagonal establishes that the BACI interaction carries information about displacement, and its distance from the upper-left corner establishes that the information is limited. The limitation is the design's, not the analyst's, and it is the reason why the cluster count matters.

## 5. Results

The central implication of the analysis is that the BACI design's power to detect displacement is governed by the cluster count, and the cluster counts typical of ECOWAS corridor research are at the low end of the range where detection is feasible. The required cluster count for a 5-percentage-point effect at 80 per cent power is 54, which is well above the 8, 16, 24 and 32 clusters in the design grid. A corridor study with 32 clusters is better powered than one with 8, but it is not adequately powered to detect the effect size that the policy literature treats as substantial.

The implication is not that corridor studies should be abandoned; it is that their null results should be interpreted with the design's power in mind.

The cluster-count constraint is structural rather than incidental. It arises from the intraclass correlation of 0.640, which implies that two-thirds of the variance is between clusters. In a setting with high between-cluster heterogeneity, adding clusters adds information slowly, and the effective sample size is much smaller than the nominal cluster count. The corridor setting is exactly such a setting: crossings differ systematically in traffic volume, enforcement intensity, and network structure, and those differences are stable over time.

The design's power is therefore limited by the heterogeneity that makes the corridor interesting as a subject of study.

The displacement hypothesis is a claim about a market-displacement effect, and the analysis shows that the effect sizes the hypothesis implies are at the edge of what the BACI design can detect. A 3-percentage-point displacement is below the minimum detectable effect at every cluster count in the design grid. A 5-percentagepoint displacement is detectable at 54 clusters, which is above the grid's upper end. An 8-percentage-point displacement is detectable at 32 clusters, which is within the grid.

The hypothesis is therefore testable only if the displacement is large, and the qualitative literature suggests that displacement is often modest and spatially diffuse. The quantitative literature's null results are therefore consistent with the displacement hypothesis, because the designs used to test it are underpowered to detect the effect sizes the hypothesis implies.

This reading of the literature has a methodological consequence. The qualitative evidence on smuggling-network adaptation, such as Meagher's (2010) account of network reorganisation and Golub's (2015) documentation of corridor porosity, is not in tension with the quantitative literature's null results. The two bodies of evidence are answering different questions with different designs, and the quantitative designs are not powered to detect the effects that the qualitative evidence describes. The appropriate response is not to privilege one body of evidence over the other but to recognise that the quantitative designs have a resolution limit, and that the limit is a property of the cluster count and the intraclass correlation.

The analysis does not establish that the BACI design is the best available design for studying border closure regimes. It establishes that the BACI design's operating characteristics impose a binding constraint on what can be learned from corridor studies with the cluster counts that the corridor supports. Alternative designs, such as computed control with a larger donor pool or the use of within-crossing variation in enforcement intensity, may have different operating characteristics. The computed-control approach that the title of the present analysis invokes is one such alternative, and its operating characteristics are a separate question that the present analysis does not resolve.

The contribution here is the characterisation of the BACI design's power surface, not a comparison of designs.

The power surface characterised in Figures 1 through 6 is a property of the declared variance structure, and the variance structure is a property of the corridor setting. A corridor with lower between-cluster heterogeneity would have a lower intraclass correlation and a more favourable power surface. A corridor with higher heterogeneity would have a less favourable one.

The analysis therefore provides a template for characterising the operating characteristics of the BACI design in any corridor setting, and the template can be applied with the variance components that the setting implies. The template's value is that it makes the design's resolution limit explicit before the study is conducted, rather than after the null result is reported.

## 6. Conclusion

The BACI design's power to detect market-displacement effects in the ECOWAS corridor is governed by the cluster count and the intraclass correlation. Under a declared variance structure with a between-cluster standard deviation of 6.0, a residual standard deviation of 4.5, and an intraclass correlation of 0.640, the design requires 54 clusters to detect a 5-percentage-point displacement effect at 80 per cent power. The cluster counts typical of corridor research, which range from 8 to 32, are below that threshold. The design's resolution limit is therefore structural, and it operates through the heterogeneity that makes the corridor a difficult setting for cluster-based inference.

The account does not resolve the displacement hypothesis, and it does not compare the BACI design with the computed-control alternatives that the title invokes. It establishes that the BACI design's operating characteristics impose a binding constraint on corridor research, and it provides a template for characterising that constraint in any corridor setting. The template's value is that it makes the design's resolution limit explicit, and it allows the analyst to distinguish a null result that is evidence of no displacement from a null result that is evidence of an underpowered design.

The policy implication is that corridor studies should report their power characteristics alongside their estimates. A study that reports a null result without reporting the minimum detectable effect at its cluster count is reporting a result that the reader cannot interpret. The minimum detectable effect curve in Figure 2 is the quantity that makes the null result interpretable, and it should be a standard feature of corridor research reporting. The analytical implication is that the displacement hypothesis should be tested with designs that are powered to detect the effect sizes it implies, and the design grid in the present analysis shows that those designs require cluster counts that the corridor setting may not support.

The tension between the hypothesis and the design is the paper's central finding, and it is a tension that the field has not yet resolved.
