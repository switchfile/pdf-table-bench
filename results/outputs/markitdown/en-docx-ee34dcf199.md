International Cooperation and Regulatory Harmonization

Dr Ana Tomičić
https://orcid.org/0000-0002-2504-4945

Abstract
Debates on the regulation of artificial intelligence in the workplace often assume the presence of a
clearly identifiable employer and a stable chain of legal responsibility. In many employment contexts,
this assumption continues to hold. In the platform economy, however, it increasingly breaks down.
This chapter examines the regulation of workplace AI through an empirical study of food delivery
work in Croatia, where labour is organised through a dual governance structure. Digital platforms
retain control over algorithmic systems that allocate work, rank performance, and impose sanctions,
while third-party aggregators formally employ workers and administer contracts, wages, and
regulatory compliance. This separation is not incidental. It operates as a governance strategy that
disperses responsibility across multiple actors and makes accountability difficult to locate in practice.
Drawing on interviews with delivery workers and union representatives, the chapter shows how this
governance arrangement produces a regulatory vacuum. No single authority can compel full
transparency over algorithmic decision-making, and no institution can enforce labour protections
across the entire governance chain. Workers experience this as income volatility, opaque discipline,
and a persistent sense that rules exist but lack an identifiable owner.
The Croatian case is analytically useful because current harmonisation efforts at the EU level,
including the Platform Work Directive, the Artificial Intelligence Act, and related national initiatives,
continue to rely on the assumption of a single, coherent employer or deployer. By contrast, dual
governance renders this assumption untenable. The chapter argues that international regulatory
harmonisation fails not primarily because legal regimes differ, but because they converge around an
outdated unit of responsibility. Meaningful coordination requires attention to the full chain of actors
involved in controlling work through AI, rather than focusing on platforms or employers in isolation.

1. Introduction

European debates on regulating artificial intelligence in the workplace increasingly frame the problem
in technical and ethical terms, focusing on transparency, explainability, and the mitigation of
algorithmic bias (European Commission, 2019; Yeung, 2018). While these concerns are important,
they rest on a more fundamental legal assumption: that there exists a clearly identifiable subject
against whom regulatory obligations can be enforced (Davidov, 2017). Existing analyses typically
treat regulatory failure as a problem of evasion, enforcement, or technological opacity; this chapter
instead locates failure at the level of legal subjecthood itself. Across EU labour law, data protection,
and emerging AI governance frameworks, regulation presumes the presence of a deployer of AI
systems, a data controller, and an employer capable of bearing responsibility for working conditions,
decision-making processes, and their consequences (Davidov, 2017; Supiot, 2017; European Data
Protection Board, 2020). Although the Artificial Intelligence Act has since entered into force, this
chapter refers, where relevant, to the earlier proposal. The reason is straightforward: the core
regulatory assumptions that structure responsibility and risk allocation did not change in any
substantive way between the proposal and the final text. For the purposes of the argument developed
here, the legal problem therefore remains the same.
Platform labour brings these assumptions under particular strain. Rather than operating through a
single organisational actor, many platform-based labour models rely on delegated governance
structures in which core employer functions are split across several entities. In the Croatian food-
delivery sector examined in this chapter, this separation is especially visible. Intermediary firms,
referred to here as aggregators, formally employ delivery workers and handle contractual and
administrative matters, while operating downstream from digital platforms that retain control over
algorithmic management.
In practice, platforms continue to determine how work is organised through algorithmic systems that
allocate orders, rank performance, and impose sanctions. Aggregators, by contrast, administer

1

contracts, wages, tax and social security contributions, and other elements of formal employment
status (De Stefano, 2016; Prassl, 2018). Unlike temporary work agencies or informal labour brokers,
these aggregators are institutionally embedded actors within the Croatian labour market. Yet they
exercise little or no influence over the algorithmic systems that shape work allocation, performance
evaluation, and disciplinary outcomes on a day-to-day basis.
At the same time, algorithmic systems are routinely framed as neutral or automated tools. This
framing further obscures where decision-making authority actually lies and makes it difficult to trace
responsibility for outcomes that directly affect working conditions (Kellogg, Valentine, & Christin,
2020). The result is a regulatory blind spot: authority is clearly exercised, but accountability becomes
difficult to locate in legal terms.
Labour law struggles to identify the employer responsible for algorithmically mediated decisions,
while data protection frameworks such as the GDPR presuppose a data controller who can
meaningfully explain, justify, and contest automated processing. When platforms deny employer
status and intermediaries deny control over algorithmic systems, neither legal regime can be fully
activated. Responsibility does not disappear, but it becomes fragmented across organisational layers
in ways that make enforcement practically impossible (Aloisi, 2022).
This chapter approaches the regulation of AI in the workplace through this structural problem of
delegated responsibility rather than through a critique of algorithmic management as such. Building
on empirical research into food-delivery platform labour, it argues that governance arrangements
separating technological control from formal employment functions undermine the foundational
assumptions of contemporary AI and labour regulation. Croatia serves here as a diagnostic case: not
because it represents an exceptional form of platform labour, but because the institutionalisation of
aggregator-based employment makes the fragmentation of responsibility unusually visible. The
Croatian case thus reveals a broader challenge confronting international efforts to regulate AI-driven
work. By examining why existing legal frameworks repeatedly fail to attach responsibility in AI-
mediated workplaces, this chapter contributes to broader debates on the limits and possibilities of
regulating AI at work across jurisdictions. This chapter therefore approaches AI governance not as a
problem of regulatory coordination or technical adjustment, but as a distributive and political
problem. The organisation of algorithmic work redistributes authority, risk, and value across actors in
ways that existing legal frameworks struggle to register. Understanding regulatory failure in this
context requires attention not only to doctrinal gaps, but to how governance structures actively shape
who bears responsibility, and who benefits from its displacement. Although the analysis is grounded
in European regulatory frameworks, the problem identified here reflects a broader pattern in
contemporary AI governance, in which international and transnational regulatory instruments
presuppose legal subjects misaligned with delegated and platform-based forms of organising work.

2. Empirical study: data and approach

This chapter draws on qualitative empirical research conducted in Croatia’s food-delivery sector
between 2023 and 2024. The study examined platform-mediated delivery work organised through
intermediary firms (aggregators), which formally employ workers while digital platforms retain
control over algorithmic systems governing task allocation, performance evaluation, and disciplinary
mechanisms.
The empirical material generated by the study includes 29 semi-structured interviews with food-
delivery workers, four focus-group sessions (6–8 participants per session, largely overlapping across
meetings) with the same group of unionised delivery workers, and interviews with union
representatives involved in organising platform labour. The repeated sessions allowed for longitudinal
discussion of evolving algorithmic practices and collective interpretation of governance changes. In
addition, a small survey was used to collect descriptive information on working arrangements,
contractual status, and income variability, providing contextual background for the qualitative
material. This chapter draws primarily on the interview and focus-group material, using the survey
data only to situate workers’ accounts.
Worker participants were recruited through a combination of union contacts, outreach via social-
media groups used by delivery workers, and snowball sampling. Union representatives were selected
on the basis of their direct involvement in organising platform workers and their engagement with
collective bargaining and regulatory processes in the delivery sector. Interviews with workers focused

2

on everyday experiences of algorithmic management. These included how work was allocated, how
income fluctuated from week to week, how performance was ranked, and how disciplinary measures
were applied. Workers were also asked about their attempts, if any, to contest decisions that affected
their access to work or income.
Interviews with union representatives addressed a different set of issues. They focused on
organisational structures, contractual arrangements, and the practical challenges that arise in
interactions with platforms, aggregators, and regulatory institutions. This distinction in focus reflected
the different positions occupied by workers and union actors within the governance of platform
labour.
In addition to formal interviews and focus groups, the analysis also draws on informal conversations
with workers during union meetings and organising activities. Documentary material, including
employment contracts, platform communications, and internal guidance circulated among workers,
was used alongside interview data. These materials helped to contextualise workers’ accounts and to
trace how authority, responsibility, and control were described and understood across organisational
actors.
All participants provided informed consent prior to taking part in the study. Participation was
voluntary, and all data were anonymised at the point of transcription. In line with applicable national
guidelines, the study did not require approval from a formal institutional ethics committee, as it
involved adult participants discussing their professional experiences and did not pose foreseeable
risks beyond routine work-related discussion.
While the empirical study generated a broader body of material, this chapter does not seek to offer a
comprehensive empirical account of platform labour in Croatia. Instead, it draws selectively on the
dataset in order to examine how delegated governance structures shape the attribution of
responsibility in AI-mediated work. Croatia serves as a particularly useful empirical setting because
aggregator-based employment in the delivery sector is relatively explicit and institutionalised. This
makes the separation between contractual employment and algorithmic control especially visible in
practice and allows the analysis to move beyond questions of misclassification toward the legal and
regulatory consequences of fragmented authority.

3. Empirical findings: dual governance in practice

The empirical material points to a consistent pattern of dual governance. Authority over work is
exercised through algorithmic systems controlled by platforms, while formal employment relations
are administered by aggregators. In practice, workers experience these two centres of control as
closely intertwined. When questions of responsibility arise, however, they become institutionally
separate in ways that matter a great deal for accountability.

3.1.

Fragmented authority

Workers repeatedly described platforms as the actors that “decide everything that matters”. This
included access to shifts, order allocation, performance ratings, and the risk of deactivation. These
decisions were widely perceived as automated and non-negotiable. They were communicated through
apps or system notifications rather than through identifiable managers or points of contact.
At the same time, aggregators were understood as responsible for contracts, payroll, scheduling
paperwork, and compliance with labour regulations. Workers therefore encountered a clear division of
roles. The entity that shaped income levels and work intensity on a daily basis was not the entity with
which they held an employment relationship.
This division generated uncertainty about where authority actually resided. While workers interacted
continuously with algorithmic systems, they were formally instructed to direct employment-related
questions to the aggregator. The resulting governance structure was experienced as paradoxical:
control over outcomes felt centralised, yet accountability appeared dispersed across multiple actors.

3.2.

Responsibility displacement

These dynamics became especially visible when workers attempted to contest decisions. Sudden
drops in order allocation, unexplained performance penalties, or temporary suspensions were common
examples. In such cases, workers reported being redirected between platforms and aggregators.

3

Platforms typically framed outcomes as system-generated and beyond human discretion. Aggregators,
in turn, claimed that they had no access to, or influence over, the algorithmic systems responsible for
those outcomes. Responsibility was rarely denied outright. Instead, it was repeatedly deferred.
The same pattern extended to data-related concerns. Workers who sought information about how
performance was evaluated, or which data points triggered sanctions, often struggled to identify which
entity controlled the relevant processing. Requests for clarification circulated between platforms and
aggregators without resolution. In this sense, rights existed formally, but no actor appeared practically
positioned to fulfil them.

3.3.

Practical consequences for workers

Workers experienced the consequences of this governance structure not as abstract legal problems,
but as forms of everyday instability. They described income unpredictability, difficulties in planning
working hours, anxiety linked to opaque performance metrics, and a persistent sense of vulnerability
to automated sanctions. Importantly, these effects were not attributed to isolated platform policies.
Instead, workers linked them to the absence of any actor who could be meaningfully engaged when
problems arose.
Several workers summarised this situation succinctly by noting that “rules exist, but belong to no
one”. This observation captures the empirical core of the chapter’s argument. Authority was clearly
exercised, yet responsibility remained elusive. The resulting insecurity was therefore not simply a
feature of platform work as such, but a consequence of how algorithmic control and employment
administration were institutionally separated.
These empirical patterns provide the foundation for the theoretical analysis that follows. Rather than
treating dual governance solely as a mechanism of labour control, the next section examines how this
organisational arrangement undermines the legal assumptions embedded in labour law, data
protection law, and emerging AI governance frameworks.

4. From dual governance to the disappearing employer

The concept of dual governance was originally developed to describe how platform labour is
organised through two distinct but interdependent centres of control (Tomičić, 2026). On the one
hand, digital platforms exercise algorithmic authority over work allocation, performance evaluation,
and disciplinary mechanisms. On the other hand, intermediary firms or aggregators manage
contractual relations, wages, and formal employment status. In this configuration, workers experience
a fragmented system of command in which control over labour is exercised without a corresponding
concentration of responsibility (De Stefano, 2016; Prassl, 2018; Kellogg, Valentine, & Christin,
2020).
In earlier analyses of labour processes (Tomičić, 2026), dual governance has primarily been used to
explain the intensification of precarity, alienation, and uncertainty in platform-mediated work. The
separation of algorithmic decision-making from contractual employment weakens workers’
bargaining power and amplifies exposure to arbitrary discipline, unpredictable income, and opaque
evaluation systems. These dynamics are well documented in the literature on algorithmic management
and platform work, where fragmentation is understood as a mechanism of labour control rather than
an accidental organisational feature (Kellogg, Valentine, & Christin, 2020; Aloisi, 2022).
This chapter departs from that interpretation by shifting the analytical focus from labour control to
legal ontology. One could interpret these arrangements as complex but ultimately reconcilable forms
of joint employment or shared control; however, the empirical material examined here suggests that,
in practice, such reconciliation remains legally unavailable.
It is true that labour law has long relied on purposive interpretation, joint employment doctrines, and
functional tests of control to address fragmented organisational arrangements. In principle, these tools
allow courts and regulators to look beyond formal contracts and identify the actor exercising effective
authority over work. However, such doctrines presuppose evidentiary access to decision-making
processes and the ability to trace control to identifiable organisational actors. In AI-mediated
governance structures, delegation actively undermines these preconditions. Algorithmic decision-
making is insulated from both workers and intermediaries, while platforms can plausibly deny

4

employer status and aggregators deny control over the systems that shape work. The result is not a
failure to apply purposive doctrines, but a structural environment in which their conditions of
operation no longer hold.

Rather than asking how dual governance reshapes workers’ subjective experiences or labour
conditions, this chapter examines what this governance structure does to the legal concept of the
employer. Contemporary labour law, data protection law, and emerging AI governance frameworks
share a foundational assumption: that decision-making power can be legally attributed to an
identifiable actor, such as an employer, data controller, provider, or deployer, capable of bearing
responsibility, providing justification, and being held liable for outcomes (Freedland & Kountouris,
2011; Davidov, 2017; Yeung, 2018; Veale & Borgesius, 2021). Dual governance systematically
undermines this premise. In platform labour organised through aggregators, the entity that controls
algorithmic systems routinely denies employer status, presenting itself as a neutral technological
intermediary rather than a labour actor. At the same time, the entity that formally employs workers
typically claims that it has no influence over the algorithmic processes that shape work intensity,
access to income, or disciplinary outcomes. Responsibility, in this configuration, is neither absent nor
accidental. It is actively displaced across organisational layers. The employer therefore persists only
as a formal designation, increasingly detached from effective control over the labour process.
From a legal perspective, this fragmentation produces an outcome that goes beyond precarity alone.
Dual governance does not simply divide managerial functions between different actors. Instead, it
erodes the employer as a coherent legal subject altogether (Tomičić, 2026). Where control over work
allocation, data processing, and discipline is exercised by different entities, each of which disclaims
responsibility for the overall system, law loses its primary point of attachment. Regulatory obligations
that presuppose a single employer, deployer, or controller cannot be meaningfully enforced when no
actor occupies that position in practice.
This transformation becomes especially visible in contexts where aggregator-based employment has
been fully institutionalised. In such cases, the separation between algorithmic authority and
administrative employment is neither informal nor hidden. It is explicitly encoded in contractual
arrangements and reinforced through organisational narratives. The result is not regulatory ambiguity
at the margins, but a structural condition in which accountability is rendered indeterminate by design.
Dual governance thus marks a shift from fragmented control toward the disappearance of the
employer as a legally actionable entity.
This reconceptualisation is central to the argument of the chapter. By treating dual governance as a
problem of legal ontology rather than labour process alone, it becomes possible to explain why even
regulatory initiatives that succeed in clarifying employment status or procedural rights often struggle
to reattach responsibility across AI-mediated governance chains (Supiot, 2017).

5. Delegation, AI, and regulatory assumptions

Delegation has long been a feature of complex labour arrangements, with employer functions
routinely distributed across managerial hierarchies, subcontracting chains, and intermediaries
(Freedland, 2003; Arthurs, 2006; Davidov, 2017). In AI-mediated workplaces, however, delegation
acquires a distinct regulatory significance, as decision-making authority is increasingly embedded in
algorithmic systems and fragmented across technical and organisational actors in ways that strain
existing models of legal accountability (Aloisi & De Stefano, 2020; Yeung, 2018; Cohen, 2019).
Delegation enables the transfer of decision-making authority across organisational layers without a
corresponding relocation of accountability, leaving responsibility formally assigned yet practically
difficult to reach (Bovens, 1998; Mashaw, 2006; Supiot, 2017). Although this dynamic predates
digital labour, AI-mediated systems significantly intensify its effects. They do so by embedding
judgment and evaluation within socio-technical infrastructures that are often opaque to both workers
and regulators (Pasquale, 2015; Yeung, 2018; Aloisi & De Stefano, 2020). What had previously been
visible as organisational choice is increasingly mediated through technical systems that obscure how
decisions are actually made.
In practice, AI systems amplify delegation in several interrelated ways. One effect is the obscuring of
human decision points. Decisions affecting work allocation, performance ranking, or disciplinary
action are frequently presented as the outputs of automated systems, rather than as outcomes shaped

5

by organisational priorities or managerial discretion (Pasquale, 2015; Yeung, 2018; Aloisi & De
Stefano, 2020; Rosenblat, 2018).
A second effect concerns the multiplication of actors involved in governance. AI-mediated
arrangements often separate system development, deployment, data control, and employment
administration across different entities. As a result, authority is distributed across technical and
organisational layers in ways that are difficult to reconstruct after the fact (Yeung, 2018; Aloisi & De
Stefano, 2020; Veale & Borgesius, 2021).
Taken together, these arrangements enable the denial of responsibility. Each actor can plausibly claim
that decisive authority lies elsewhere, whether in the design of the system, its deployment, or its day-
to-day operation. Delegation therefore becomes more than an organisational convenience. It operates
as a mechanism for diffusing accountability across governance chains (Bovens, 2007; Supiot, 2017;
Pasquale, 2015; Elish, 2019).
Existing regulatory frameworks are poorly equipped to address this configuration because they rest on
incompatible assumptions. AI regulation, in particular, is primarily oriented toward technological
systems. It seeks to govern risk by classifying applications, assigning obligations to providers and
deployers, and mandating transparency and oversight mechanisms (EU AI Act, 2024; Veale &
Borgesius, 2021; Yeung, 2018). This system-centred approach sits uneasily alongside labour and data
protection regimes that presuppose identifiable social actors capable of bearing responsibility. Labour
law, by contrast, regulates social relations of work. It presupposes an employer who organises
production, exercises authority, and can be held responsible for working conditions and outcomes
(Kahn-Freund, 1977; Freedland & Kountouris, 2011; Davidov, 2017). Delegation disrupts this logic.
When authority is exercised through AI systems operated by one entity while employment relations
are administered by another, neither labour law’s relational framework nor AI regulation’s system-
centred approach can fully attach responsibility (Supiot, 2017; Aloisi & De Stefano, 2020; Yeung,
2018).

Policy analyses of AI governance increasingly recognise delegation as a source of legal vulnerability,
as the dispersion of authority across developers, deployers, and intermediaries complicates
accountability and weakens enforcement (Yeung, 2018; European Commission, 2021a; Veale &
Borgesius, 2021). Rather than creating entirely new forms of harm, AI systems often expose and
intensify gaps between existing regulatory regimes, allowing decisions with significant social and
economic consequences to fall outside enforceable oversight (Pasquale, 2015; Yeung, 2018; European
Commission, 2021b; Veale & Borgesius, 2021).
While the Croatian food-delivery sector provides a particularly clear illustration due to the
formalisation of aggregator-based employment, the underlying logic is not jurisdiction-specific.
Delegated AI governance produces similar regulatory blind spots wherever technological control,
contractual authority, and data management are institutionally separated. Croatia thus functions as a
diagnostic case that renders visible a more general condition: the misalignment between regulatory
assumptions and contemporary modes of organising AI-mediated work.
This dynamic mirrors the governance structures observed in platform labour. Efforts to regulate
algorithms, mandate transparency, or reclassify employment status presuppose stable points of
intervention that delegation systematically erodes. As long as decision-making authority can be
exercised without legal ownership of its consequences, regulatory regimes addressing AI in the
workplace will remain partial and fragile.

6. Where regulation breaks: three regimes, one fault line

The difficulties identified in the regulation of AI-mediated work are often discussed as failures of
specific legal regimes: labour law is said to be outdated, data protection law too procedural, and AI
regulation overly technical. While each of these critiques captures part of the problem, they obscure a
more basic point. In systems organised through delegated governance, that addressee dissolves.

6.1.

Labour law: authority without an employer

In AI-mediated platform labour organised through aggregators, the assumption that responsibility can
be anchored in a single employer no longer holds. Instead, authority over work allocation,

6

performance evaluation, and discipline is exercised through algorithmic systems controlled by one
actor, while employment relations are formally administered by another. As a result, labour law does
not simply misclassify workers or misidentify control; it loses its point of attachment. Even where
workers are formally recognised as employees, the employer lacks effective control over the systems
shaping their work. Labour law thus fails not because its protections are inadequate, but because the
entity it seeks to regulate no longer coincides with the locus of authority. This fragmentation also has
collective consequences. During interviews, trade union representatives repeatedly described the
difficulty of identifying a counterpart with whom to negotiate working conditions in platform-based
delivery work. Employment is dispersed across a large number of intermediary firms, many of which
employ only a small number of workers, while effective control over work allocation and discipline
remains with the platform. As a result, collective bargaining is structurally impeded, not because
unions are absent, but because there is no identifiable employer exercising effective authority.

6.2.

AI regulation: system-centred obligations without social reach
By contrast, AI regulation approaches governance from a different starting point. Its primary focus is
on technological systems rather than on social relations of work. Most risk-based frameworks proceed
by classifying AI applications, assigning obligations to providers or deployers, and requiring a set of
procedural safeguards, including transparency measures, forms of human oversight, and
documentation requirements (European Commission, 2021b; Yeung, 2018).
This approach is internally coherent, but poorly aligned with the organisation of AI-mediated labour.
When algorithmic systems structure work but are operated by entities that disclaim employer status,
AI regulation can address technical risks without engaging the social consequences of those systems.
Obligations attach to the use of technology, not to the organisation of work as such. The result is a
form of regulatory containment: systems may be audited or documented, yet decisions affecting
income, discipline, and security remain outside enforceable labour protections. AI regulation thus
governs the artefact while leaving the employment relationship structurally unaddressed.

6.3.

Data protection law: rights without a controller

Data protection law appears, at first glance, to offer a corrective to both regimes. Procedural rights of
access, explanation, and contestation provide a potential entry point for challenging opaque
algorithmic decisions. In practice, however, these rights depend on the existence of a clearly
identifiable data controller.
In delegated platform labour arrangements, data relevant to working conditions is processed across
multiple actors. Platforms control algorithmic systems while denying responsibility for employment-
related processing; aggregators administer employment while claiming limited access to, or influence
over, algorithmic decision-making. As a result, GDPR rights remain formally intact but practically
unenforceable. Workers can invoke rights, but cannot locate an actor capable of fulfilling them. Data
protection law does not fail because its safeguards are weak, but because its addressee cannot be
coherently identified.

6.4.

One fault line, multiple failures

Taken together, these failures reveal a shared structural problem. Labour law presumes an employer,
AI regulation presumes a deployer, and data protection law presumes a controller. In AI-mediated
labour organised through delegated governance, none of these figures exists as a unified subject.
Authority, employment, and data control are distributed across actors who each satisfy only part of the
legal criteria for responsibility.
This misalignment explains why regulatory reforms often produce limited effects. Strengthening
labour protections, refining AI risk classifications, or expanding data subject rights cannot succeed if
the organisational structures of work systematically prevent responsibility from attaching. Regulatory
failure here stems less from normative gaps than from the erosion of identifiable legal addressees.
The remainder of the chapter builds on this diagnosis. Rather than reiterating the failure of individual
regimes, it examines the political-economic consequences of delegated AI governance and considers
what forms of regulatory reasoning might be capable of reattaching responsibility across fragmented
chains of control.

7. Data, control, and the limits of GDPR in platform labour

In algorithmically mediated workplaces, data protection law increasingly functions as a form of
labour regulation (Aloisi & De Stefano, 2020). Where labour law struggles to capture opaque
decision-making processes, the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) offers workers a set of
procedural rights that can, in principle, be used to interrogate algorithmic management. Rights of
access, explanation, and contestation provide one of the few legal avenues through which workers
may seek insight into how decisions affecting income, performance evaluation, or discipline are made
(Wachter et al., 2017). In this sense, GDPR functions not only as a privacy framework, but also as a
de facto labour right in data-driven work environments. Where labour law struggles to reach opaque
decision-making processes, data protection rules offer one of the few procedural entry points through
which workers can attempt to interrogate algorithmic management.
At the same time, GDPR rests on a foundational assumption that closely mirrors that of labour law:
the existence of a clearly identifiable data controller. The regulation presumes that responsibility for
determining the purposes and means of data processing can be assigned to a specific entity. That
entity is expected to respond meaningfully to data subject requests, ensure transparency, and bear
liability in cases of non-compliance. In platform labour organised through aggregator models, this
assumption is systematically undermined.
In such arrangements, platforms typically assert control over algorithmic systems while denying
responsibility for employment-related data processing, often presenting themselves as technology
providers or neutral intermediaries. Aggregators, for their part, manage payroll, contracts, and formal
employment relationships, but frequently claim that they lack access to, or influence over, the
algorithmic systems that structure work allocation and performance evaluation. The result is a
fragmentation of data processing relevant to working conditions across multiple actors, none of whom
fully satisfy the role of controller in practice, even where they may do so formally.
Empirical material from the Croatian food-delivery sector illustrates how this fragmentation is
experienced on the ground. Workers reported difficulty identifying which entity controlled the data
used to allocate tasks, assess performance, or trigger sanctions. Requests for information were often
redirected between platforms and aggregators, with each actor disclaiming responsibility for the
relevant processing activities. What emerges here is not the absence of rights, but their practical
inoperability. GDPR protections remain formally intact, yet lack a coherent and enforceable
addressee.
This pattern reinforces the broader argument developed in the chapter. Just as labour law falters when
it cannot identify an employer exercising effective authority, data protection law struggles when it
cannot locate a controller capable of bearing responsibility. In this respect, the difficulties encountered
under GDPR mirror the structural misalignment identified above.
Seen from this perspective, weakening data protection safeguards or narrowing their scope has
consequences that extend well beyond privacy. When GDPR’s capacity to function as a procedural
check on algorithmic power is eroded, workers lose one of the last remaining tools available for
contesting opaque governance arrangements. In AI-mediated labour systems characterised by
delegated responsibility, the erosion of data protection therefore directly reinforces the conditions
under which accountability becomes structurally unattainable. What appears as legal indeterminacy
also has clear distributive effects, shaping where risk accumulates and where value ultimately flows.

8. Unequal exchange in platform labour

Legal indeterminacy in AI-mediated labour governance is not neutral. When no responsible legal
subject can be clearly identified, the consequences extend beyond enforcement difficulties,
systematically shifting risk downward and value upward. Where responsibility cannot attach,
uncertainty, volatility, and compliance burdens are borne by workers, while platforms and
intermediaries retain the benefits of algorithmic coordination without corresponding accountability.
The governance structures analysed in the previous sections do not merely generate regulatory
ambiguity; they stabilise a particular distribution of value, risk, and responsibility. To capture this
distributive pattern, this subsection briefly mobilises the concept of unequal exchange, not as a

8

comprehensive theoretical framework, but as a diagnostic lens for understanding the political-
economic effects of legally fragmented governance in AI-mediated labour.
In platform labour organised through delegation, value is extracted through algorithmic coordination
and data-driven optimisation, while the risks associated with volatility, uncertainty, and compliance
are systematically displaced onto workers. Platforms benefit from the scalability and efficiency of
algorithmic management without assuming the responsibilities normally associated with an employer.
Aggregators, by contrast, absorb formal employment status while exercising little control over the
systems that shape work intensity or income. Workers are left to bear the cumulative effects of this
arrangement. These include unpredictable earnings, exposure to automated sanctions, and a limited
capacity to contest decisions that directly affect their livelihoods.
Taken together, this configuration produces an asymmetrical exchange. Labour power is rendered
continuously available, while security and accountability are systematically withheld. The purpose of
introducing the concept of unequal exchange here is not to broaden the empirical scope of the chapter.
Rather, it serves to clarify the distributive consequences of the legal indeterminacy already
documented in the preceding sections.
Importantly, this asymmetry cannot be adequately explained as a market failure or as the outcome of
individual bargaining weakness. It is better understood as the result of institutional design.
Governance structures that separate algorithmic control from legal responsibility enable value to flow
upward while risk is displaced downward. As recent Marxist work on unequal exchange has
emphasised, such patterns tend to persist when institutional arrangements normalise asymmetry rather
than correct it (Lauesen, 2025). In the context examined here, law does not simply fail to
counterbalance unequal exchange. Through its reliance on stable legal subjects that no longer
correspond to organisational realities, it inadvertently contributes to its reproduction.
The role of AI in this process is therefore specific and limited. AI does not create unequal exchange in
platform labour.
Instead, it locks it in by embedding asymmetrical governance relations into automated systems that
operate beyond identifiable points of accountability. Algorithmic management renders the terms of
exchange opaque, standardised, and difficult to contest, while delegated responsibility prevents
workers from locating an actor against whom claims might be addressed. Unequal exchange thus
emerges as the effect of legal indeterminacy rather than its cause.
Seen in this light, the distributive consequences of AI-mediated labour governance cannot be
addressed through technical adjustments to algorithms alone. As long as fragmented governance
structures dissolve the legal subjects required for enforcement, the asymmetrical allocation of value
and risk will remain structurally stable. Unequal exchange in platform labour is therefore best
understood not as an aberration of digital markets, but as a predictable outcome of regulatory
frameworks that fail to engage with delegation and responsibility across organisational chains.

9. Why international harmonisation fails

Efforts to regulate AI in the workplace increasingly rely on international and supranational
harmonisation. At the EU level, the Platform Work Directive, the Artificial Intelligence Act, and the
GDPR are often presented as complementary instruments capable of addressing the challenges posed
by algorithmic management (European Commission, 2021a; European Union, 2024). Taken together,
they promise coherent protection across labour, data, and technology governance. Yet the analysis
developed in this chapter suggests that harmonisation fails for a more fundamental reason than
regulatory inconsistency.
Each of these frameworks presupposes a stable object of regulation. The Platform Work Directive
assumes the existence of an employer who organises work and can be held responsible for
employment conditions. The AI Act assumes the presence of a deployer who exercises meaningful
control over the use of AI systems and is able to ensure compliance with risk management and
oversight obligations. GDPR, by contrast, assumes a data controller who determines the purposes and
means of processing and can respond effectively to data subject rights. Within their respective
frameworks, these assumptions are internally coherent. The difficulty emerges when they are applied
to labour arrangements organised through delegation, where control and responsibility no longer
coincide.

Comparative experiences across EU member states reinforce this conclusion. Spain’s Rider Law, for
instance, sought to address misclassification by introducing a presumption that platform workers are
employees (Lamannis, 2023). While this intervention clarified employment status, it did not resolve
questions of algorithmic accountability or data control where governance functions continued to be
distributed across complex corporate structures. Similarly, regulatory and judicial initiatives in Italy,
including experiments with algorithmic audits and transparency obligations, have aimed to improve
visibility into decision-making processes (Aloisi & De Stefano, 2020). In practice, however, they have
struggled to attach responsibility in situations where platforms and intermediaries can plausibly deny
comprehensive control. These cases should not be read as failures of ambition or enforcement. Rather,
they expose the limits of regulatory strategies that continue to rely on entity-based responsibility.
The Croatian case discussed in this chapter makes this limitation particularly visible because
aggregator-based employment is explicitly institutionalised. At the same time, the underlying problem
is not specific to Croatia. Wherever AI-mediated work is organised through delegated governance
chains, regulatory regimes that presuppose a single legal subject encounter a similar impasse.
Harmonisation across legal domains cannot succeed if it repeatedly reproduces the same ontological
assumption at multiple levels.
From this perspective, the central challenge confronting international AI governance is not primarily
one of coordination between regulatory instruments. It is a problem of conceptual alignment with
contemporary forms of organising work. As long as regulation targets employers, deployers, or
controllers as discrete entities, rather than addressing responsibility across governance chains,
harmonisation will remain partial. The persistence of regulatory blind spots in platform labour
therefore reflects not a lack of rules, but a mismatch between regulatory design and the organisational
realities of AI-mediated production.

10. Regulatory implications: from entity-based to chain-based responsibility
The analysis developed in this chapter suggests that prevailing regulatory approaches to AI in the
workplace falter because they remain anchored to entity-based responsibility. Labour law, data
protection law, and AI governance frameworks all proceed from the assumption that accountability
can be attached to a single, identifiable subject. In AI-mediated labour organised through delegated
governance, this assumption no longer holds. Addressing the resulting regulatory blind spots therefore
requires a shift in focus, away from discrete entities and toward governance chains.
One implication of this shift concerns liability. Rather than attempting to locate responsibility within a
single employer, deployer, or controller, regulatory frameworks could recognise joint responsibility
across delegation chains. A concrete mechanism would be the introduction of a rebuttable
presumption of joint liability in situations where multiple actors jointly shape core aspects of working
conditions through algorithmic systems. While the Platform Work Directive introduces a rebuttable
presumption of employment, it continues to seek a single employer. By contrast, a presumption of
joint liability would reflect the organisational realities of platform labour more closely. Where a
platform controls work allocation, evaluation, or discipline through AI systems, and an intermediary
administers formal employment, both actors could be presumed jointly responsible for labour and data
protection outcomes, unless they can demonstrate a clear separation of control and influence. Such an
approach would not eliminate organisational complexity, but it would limit the ability to displace
responsibility through contractual design alone.
A similar adjustment could be made within data protection law through a more assertive use of joint
controller determinations. Where multiple actors jointly determine the purposes and means of data
processing relevant to working conditions, they could be treated as joint controllers by default. This
would help restore an enforceable addressee for procedural rights, without requiring workers to
reconstruct opaque governance arrangements on a case-by-case basis.
A further implication concerns enforcement. Even where joint responsibility exists in principle,
fragmented governance structures weaken enforcement when regulatory authorities operate in
isolation (Yeung, 2018). One response would be the institutionalisation of joint labour and data
protection inspections in AI-mediated workplaces. Labour inspectorates and data protection
authorities already examine overlapping aspects of the same governance systems, but rarely
coordinate their interventions. Joint inspections would make it possible to assess algorithmic

10

management as a composite phenomenon, linking working conditions, decision-making processes,
and data practices within a single supervisory procedure.
In addition, platforms and intermediaries could be subject to targeted disclosure obligations toward
labour inspectorates. Such obligations would require them to identify the actors involved in
algorithmic decision-making, data processing, and employment administration. This would not entail
full algorithmic transparency, but it would establish a minimum level of organisational visibility,
enabling regulators to determine where responsibility should attach across governance chains.
Taken together, these measures do not point toward a single legislative solution. Rather, they illustrate
how regulatory reasoning could be reoriented away from fictional responsibility and toward effective
control. As long as regulation remains centred on individual entities, AI-mediated delegation will
continue to generate accountability gaps. A chain-based approach to responsibility offers a way to
realign regulatory frameworks with the organisational realities of platform labour, making
harmonisation across legal domains substantively meaningful rather than merely formal.

11. Conclusion: AI governance as a problem of political economy

This chapter has shown that the regulatory difficulties surrounding AI in platform labour cannot be
adequately explained as failures of enforcement or deficiencies in existing legal instruments. The
disappearance of the legal subject in AI-mediated labour is not technologically inevitable, but
contingent on regulatory choices that permit responsibility to be displaced across organisational
layersi. The Croatian food-delivery sector does not constitute an institutional outlier. Rather, it makes
visible a governance pattern that is becoming increasingly common wherever AI-mediated work is
organised through delegated chains of algorithmic control and formal employment. In such
arrangements, the legal subjects presupposed by labour law, data protection law, and emerging AI
regulation no longer align with the organisational realities of work.
The accountability gaps that result are structurally produced. They arise from governance models that
separate effective control over labour from legal responsibility for its consequences, enabling the
extraction of value while displacing risk, uncertainty, and compliance burdens onto workers. AI
systems do not generate these asymmetries on their own. Rather, they stabilise and routinise them by
embedding fragmented authority into automated decision-making processes that operate beyond
identifiable points of accountability. For this reason, the regulatory challenge examined in this chapter
is best understood as a problem of political economy, rather than as a purely technical shortcoming of
AI governance.
As long as regulatory frameworks remain anchored to entity-based responsibility, efforts to govern AI
in the workplace are likely to produce only limited effects. Recognising these structural limits does
not render regulatory intervention futile. Instead, it underscores the need for approaches that move
beyond entity-based assumptions when addressing AI governance in work settings. Harmonisation
across legal domains cannot succeed if it repeatedly reproduces the same premise: that responsibility
can be attached to a single employer, deployer, or controller. Where control over work is distributed
across platforms, aggregators, and technical systems, regulation must engage directly with these
organisational chains rather than with their formal representations alone.
The critique developed in this chapter is therefore directed at the persistence of fictional responsibility
within contemporary regulatory reasoning. Reframing AI governance as a problem of political
economy makes it possible to analyse responsibility where it is actually exercised, rather than where it
is merely nominally assigned. Only by confronting how value, risk, and authority are distributed in
practice within AI-mediated labour can regulation move beyond symbolic compliance and toward
forms of accountability that are genuinely enforceable.
