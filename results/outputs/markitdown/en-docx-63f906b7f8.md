Abstract
Background: Epidemic surveillance along East African borders links infectious-disease detection to authority over movement, markets
and citizenship.
Objective: This article investigates how governments can coordinate outbreak control without making border security an instrument of
indiscriminate exclusion.
Methods: A comparative, documentary mixed-methods synthesis examines published World Health Organization and Uganda
government outbreak data, with attention to Sudan virus disease in Uganda, mpox in the African Region, and cholera in South Sudan
and neighbouring countries. Descriptive rates and changes are recalculated only where case definitions and reporting cut-offs permit.
Results: Uganda’s 2025 Sudan virus disease outbreak comprised 14 cases (12 confirmed and two probable), four deaths, and 534
monitored contacts. The WHO African Region reported 10,953 confirmed mpox cases in 2024 by 3 November and 14,849 by 1 December,
demonstrating rapidly evolving surveillance totals rather than a controlled trend. South Sudan reported 14,190 cholera cases and 122
deaths between January and August 2026.
Conclusions: Timely surveillance and information exchange require trusted, interoperable institutions; published aggregate figures
cannot isolate the causal effect of border closures or specific policing practices. This paper develops a testable governance model
combining timeliness, proportionality, reciprocity and accountability.
Keywords: border health; epidemic surveillance; South Sudan; Uganda; mpox; cholera; Sudan virus disease; regional security; public
authority; transnational cooperation.
1. Introduction
Cross-border outbreaks are not simply biological events moving across lines on a map. They are social and administrative crises that
expose disagreements over who may move, who must report symptoms, which authority controls a crossing, and which agency pays for
testing and care. A person seeking treatment, a trader crossing for a market day and a displaced household escaping conflict encounter
the border in sharply different ways. Surveillance that ignores these differences can increase concealment, delay presentation and
reduce the credibility of public-health messaging.
East Africa offers an unusually instructive setting. Population mobility crosses formal points of entry and informal pathways, while
policing, customs, local councils, humanitarian actors and health services work through overlapping mandates. In stable periods those
overlaps may facilitate practical negotiation; during epidemics they can create legal uncertainty and inconsistent restrictions. The
analytical problem is therefore not whether a state possesses the formal power to impose health measures, but when coercive power
adds public-health value and when it creates avoidable vulnerability.

The empirical cases considered here should not be collapsed into one homogeneous epidemic. Uganda’s 2025 Sudan virus disease
outbreak was comparatively small in case count but required intensive contact tracing. Mpox reporting in the WHO African Region
covered multiple states and was conditioned by laboratory capacity and evolving case definitions. Cholera in South Sudan involved
sustained disease burden against a backdrop of displacement and water, sanitation and hygiene constraints. The variation allows the
article to examine institutional mechanisms without pretending that disease-specific rates are directly comparable.
This article has four aims: to identify the documented magnitude and reporting characteristics of selected outbreaks; to explain how
border measures redistribute authority and risk; to derive operational indicators for effective cross-border cooperation; and to propose
a credible empirical design for future testing. Its contribution is a governance argument rather than a claim that the documentary
evidence reveals causal effects. It joins quantitative description to a narrative explanation of why cooperation depends on more than
border guards and laboratory equipment.
2. Theoretical and institutional framework
2.1 Securitisation and proportionality
The securitisation perspective explains why exceptional threats justify extraordinary rules, but it also warns that emergency
classification can expand discretion without clear limits. Border screening may be warranted when it detects infectious cases and
connects patients to care. It becomes counterproductive when it treats mobility itself as culpability. A proportionality test therefore asks
whether the health benefit is evidence-based, whether a less restrictive alternative exists, whether the restriction is reviewed, and
whether appeal or redress remains accessible.
For comparative research, this mechanism should be measured alongside resources and outbreak severity rather than assumed from
policy documents alone. Official directives reveal rules on paper, whereas interviews, facility logs and crossing observations are needed
to establish how the rules were experienced by travellers and local providers.
2.2 Polycentric public authority
Surveillance depends on actors operating at multiple levels: national ministries, district health teams, laboratories, immigration
agencies, humanitarian organisations, community networks and international institutions. These actors do not merely implement one
vertical command. They negotiate jurisdiction, share scarce transport and communication resources, and sometimes compete over data
ownership. Polycentric coordination works best when responsibilities and minimum information standards are mutually recognised.
For comparative research, this mechanism should be measured alongside resources and outbreak severity rather than assumed from
policy documents alone. Official directives reveal rules on paper, whereas interviews, facility logs and crossing observations are needed
to establish how the rules were experienced by travellers and local providers.
2.3 Trust, reporting and the detection paradox
Improved surveillance can initially increase reported cases, not because transmission rises but because previously unseen infections
become visible. This detection paradox complicates performance assessment: fewer reported cases do not necessarily mean stronger
border controls. The appropriate measures include testing accessibility, reporting delay, investigation coverage and linkage to
treatment, as well as incidence. A rise in notifications following improved outreach may be consistent with better public protection.
For comparative research, this mechanism should be measured alongside resources and outbreak severity rather than assumed from
policy documents alone. Official directives reveal rules on paper, whereas interviews, facility logs and crossing observations are needed
to establish how the rules were experienced by travellers and local providers.
2.4 Regional reciprocity
A useful cross-border compact has reciprocal obligations. Authorities on one side notify their counterparts promptly, while the
receiving jurisdiction guarantees that notification will not automatically result in punishment of patients or withholding of essential
services. Reciprocal arrangements also require data safeguards: nominal records should be shared only when necessary for a lawful
public-health purpose, with clear retention and access rules.

policy documents alone. Official directives reveal rules on paper, whereas interviews, facility logs and crossing observations are needed
to establish how the rules were experienced by travellers and local providers.

2.5 Normative anchors
The International Health Regulations (2005), as amended, frame obligations around detecting, assessing, notifying and responding to
risks while seeking to avoid unnecessary interference with international traffic and trade. They do not mandate blanket closures as an
automatic response to every outbreak. Operational decisions should reflect the pathogen, transmission route and prevailing evidence;
policy instruments that are useful for one disease may be unjustified for another.

3. Data and methods
The study uses a structured documentary comparison rather than newly collected field observations. Core primary sources are WHO
disease-outbreak notices, WHO Africa mpox bulletins and weekly cholera reports, together with Uganda Ministry of Health statements.
Evidence was selected for transparent dates, numerical definitions and relevance to cross-border governance. Sources are reported
with their original cut-off dates; later cumulative totals are not substituted silently for earlier values.
The statistical component is descriptive. We calculate reported case fatality as deaths divided by reported cases multiplied by 100, and
temporal increases as (later cumulative count − earlier count) divided by earlier count multiplied by 100. These calculations concern
reported surveillance data, not population infection fatality rates. Differences between geographical coverage, laboratory confirmation
and probable case inclusion preclude pooling disease-specific case-fatality ratios into a single regional estimate.
Analysis proceeds through four domains: detection and notification, cross-border coordination, exercise of security powers, and
protection of affected populations. Narrative evidence is used to identify plausible causal pathways and rival interpretations. Because
we have no individual-level interviews, journey-level crossing records or harmonised policy-timing database, the study cannot infer
that a border policy directly caused a particular mortality or case-count change.
An empirical follow-on design is offered to make the framework falsifiable. Its suggested unit of observation is the border crossing-
week, nested within district and country. Prospective variables include time from notification to investigation, laboratory turnaround,
proportion of referrals completed, hours of crossing restriction, essential-travel exemption rates, complaint volume and traveller-
reported trust. Pre-registration should define outcomes and confounders before data are analysed.

5. Narrative synthesis: where disease control meets political power
5.1 The border as a surveillance network rather than a wall
A purely territorial conception of epidemic response prioritises keeping pathogens outside a jurisdiction. Yet exposure, incubation and
informal mobility patterns mean biological risk is not exhausted by the formal port of entry. A network conception instead emphasises
interoperable notification, continuity of care and transport of specimens. Health posts can become useful nodes of detection when
individuals believe that disclosing symptoms will lead to assistance rather than indiscriminate detention or punitive treatment.
A field-based test should therefore compare formal policy with everyday implementation. Researchers would need timestamped
notifications, clinic referral registers and interviews at both sides of selected crossings. They should ask which actors were informed,
which travellers received exemptions, and whether information could be acted upon without breaching confidentiality. The absence of
such microdata in the present analysis is acknowledged rather than filled with fabricated testimony.
5.2 The paradox of exceptional enforcement
Emergency powers can accelerate inspection and data exchange, but blunt enforcement may displace travel to less visible crossings. A
reduction in recorded movement through an official gate might represent successful compliance, displacement of routes or declining
economic activity; the administrative count alone cannot distinguish the interpretations. Evaluations need mobility proxies, interviews
and information about changes in lawful crossing access.

5.3 Trade, food security and proportionality
A border restriction may interrupt essential trade and complicate healthcare for patients who routinely use facilities across an
international frontier. A defensible decision framework weighs expected transmission prevention against interruption to medicines,
food distribution and income. The relevant counterfactual is not unrestricted movement without surveillance, but less restrictive
interventions such as targeted screening, referral pathways, risk communication and structured exemptions.

5.4 Displacement and unequal exposure
South Sudan’s cholera burden underscores the importance of crowding, service access and water systems. Displaced people often face

response may intensify those barriers unless health authorities coordinate with humanitarian services and community-based
organisations. Not all displacement-associated transmission is attributable to crossing an international border.

5.5 The politics of numbers
Published counts are administrative products. Confirmed cases depend on access to testing, probable cases depend on case definition,
deaths depend on recording practices, and contacts depend on interview quality. Consequently, the best governance signal may be a set
of linked indicators rather than a single case total. Comparing raw totals between outbreaks with different diagnostics can falsely

5.7 Data exchange without surveillance overreach
Sharing personal identifiers can be necessary for contact tracing, but indefinite retention or generalised law-enforcement access can

National authorities retain responsibility for their populations, yet pathogen circulation makes unilateral success contingent on

6. Proposed quantitative and qualitative research extension
6.1 Units and sampling
Select matched crossing points in Uganda–South Sudan, Kenya–Uganda and other relevant corridors, balancing high-volume official
gates with accessible community pathways. Observe crossing-week records over comparable surveillance periods and stratify by
pathogen and outbreak phase. A priori power calculations depend on observed baseline notification delays and within-crossing
clustering; arbitrary sample sizes would be inappropriate.
The resulting dataset should preserve a public codebook, versioned source references and an auditable distinction between missing
observations and recorded zeros. Where national totals have been retrospectively revised, the analysis should retain the original
vintage alongside the corrected release. This would permit external readers to understand exactly which figures underlie each
visualisation and estimate.

7. Discussion
The findings challenge three convenient assumptions. First, the effectiveness of surveillance cannot be reduced to counting people
screened. The Uganda Sudan virus disease episode demonstrates that contact tracing may involve hundreds of individuals even when
the reported number of cases remains low. Second, stronger detection may increase reported case counts in the short term, making
simplistic rankings of administrations misleading. Third, public-health authority is not synonymous with unrestricted security
discretion; timely care and credible communication are operational dimensions of state capacity.
The comparison also illustrates the danger of treating all transboundary pathogens as equivalent. Sudan virus disease requires case
identification and contact management under a specific incubation and transmission profile. Mpox surveillance depends on testing,
case definitions and community engagement. Cholera is inseparable from safe water, sanitation and treatment access. A border regime
built for only one threat may inadequately address the others or impose disproportionate costs.
An original conceptual contribution is the reciprocal surveillance compact: a set of measurable commitments joining alert timeliness to
clinical referral, mobility safeguards, data minimisation and accountable oversight. Unlike an abstract plea for regional unity, the
compact specifies observable obligations. Its performance remains a hypothesis requiring longitudinal implementation evidence, not a
statistical effect demonstrated by the country-level figures in this manuscript.
For East African institutions, meaningful cooperation should involve harmonised alert vocabulary, training at paired crossings, shared
simulation exercises and community-level monitoring of the effects of restrictive measures. Such initiatives should be assessed not by
meeting counts alone but by changes in notification delays, referral completion and patient safety, with attention to unequal
consequences for displaced and undocumented populations.
8. Limitations
The study synthesises aggregate, published information rather than original respondent or facility data. WHO publications use differing
reporting windows and disease-specific case definitions, and some counts are retrospectively revised. Certain cross-country

comparisons are illustrative of reporting structures rather than like-for-like measures of infection risk. No causal association between a
particular border intervention and epidemic outcomes is claimed. Proposed regression models, indicators and field instruments require
ethical approval, feasibility testing and access to independently verifiable observations.
9. Conclusion and policy implications
Cross-border health surveillance in East Africa is a problem of institutional trust and legitimate authority as well as epidemiology.
Uganda’s 2025 Sudan virus disease outbreak and regional mpox and cholera data illuminate the demands placed on case detection,
reporting systems and health services; they do not provide a licence to infer that more coercive border control is necessarily more
effective. A credible model combines rapid communication, proportionate restrictions, continuity of care and protection of individual
information. Future research should test whether reciprocal agreements improve referral timeliness and equitable service access under
real outbreak conditions.
10. Implementation roadmap and evaluation criteria
10.1 First 90 days: map authority and referral routes
A feasible preparatory phase would identify paired facilities, district surveillance focal persons, laboratory transport pathways, customs
and immigration decision-makers, and civil-society liaison contacts at each crossing. For every existing memorandum, the coordinating
team should record the official signatories, current operational contacts, emergency number, minimum alert content and
acknowledgement rule. This baseline exercise should avoid inventing an entirely new command hierarchy where one already
functions. Instead, it should reveal duplicated approvals and gaps between written arrangements and actual practice. Simulation
exercises should include ordinary travellers, undocumented patients and time-sensitive medical referrals so that the rights implications
of emergency powers become visible before an outbreak.
Measurement quality is part of implementation: an independent audit should compare a sample of dashboard entries against
contemporaneous registers, document discrepancies and revise procedures without replacing the original figures invisibly. This
protects the integrity of both programme management and future peer-reviewed research.
10.2 Months four to six: rehearse the alert-to-care chain
Paired border health teams could conduct structured tabletop exercises using non-identifiable scenarios for a suspected case, a
laboratory-confirmed result and an exposure discovered after travel. The exercise should timestamp alert receipt, risk assessment,
counterpart notification, clinical referral and feedback. Debriefs should distinguish avoidable administrative waiting time from time
required for testing or clinical stabilisation. A shared after-action report should record actions agreed and persons responsible, but
need not contain real patient information. Where power or mobile-network outages affect communication, an agreed backup channel
should be tested and documented.

10.4 Governance safeguards and escalation
The cross-border compact should distinguish clinical decisions from border-enforcement decisions. Health practitioners must be able to
recommend care without automatic punitive referral. Restrictions need a written legal basis, review date, reason-giving and an
accessible route for complaints. Data-sharing officers should maintain access logs and conduct periodic deletion reviews. An escalation
protocol must specify which agency resolves disagreement when neighbouring authorities classify the same event differently, and how
urgent patient referral continues during that disagreement. These controls do not weaken disease protection; they make cooperation
more predictable and therefore easier to sustain.
Measurement quality is part of implementation: an independent audit should compare a sample of dashboard entries against
contemporaneous registers, document discrepancies and revise procedures without replacing the original figures invisibly. This
protects the integrity of both programme management and future peer-reviewed research.
10.5 What would count as a policy success
The proposed indicators should register joint improvements in three dimensions: verified shortening of alert-to-referral time,
maintenance of access to essential care across mobility-status categories, and reduction in unjustified restrictive practices. No single
composite index should hide deterioration in equity behind gains in administrative speed. Policymakers should require a minimum
acceptable score in each domain before declaring a pilot successful. External researchers can then use phased implementation and
comparable sites to assess whether the improvements persist after initial training support is withdrawn.
