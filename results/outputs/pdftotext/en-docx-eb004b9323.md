Seeds, Screens and Indigenous Management: Women’s Agrarian Resource
Governance in India’s Indo-Burma Frontier
Sonikaa, HSS Dept., BITS, Pilani, India
Prof. Sushila Shekhawatb, HSS Dept., BITS, Pilani, India

Abstract
The management practices of indigenous women are typically discussed using the frames of
conservation and activism. However, this framing overlooks a management aspect that pervades
the nature of the work. Women in the hill societies of the Indo-Burma frontier in India’s Northeast,
and in the two documentaries analyzed in this research, manage critical elements of the agrarian
commons. For example, they determine which crop varieties will be conserved, planted, sown and
saved; how crops will be organized in space; when to leave soils and fields fallow; etc. These
practices promote agro-biodiversity and thus provide the foundation upon which local food
security is established. As monoculturantations, oil palm expansion, mining, large dams and
variable climatic conditions increasingly impact hill ecosystems and cycles of shifting cultivation
are declining, the significance of these practices has increased. Therefore, this research views these
practices not as activism for the environment solely, but as an Indigenous Management System.
Indigenous women are recognized throughout much of the literature on indigenous women as the
primary custodians of ecological knowledge. Nevertheless, their decision-making authority
continues to remain primarily unaccounted for. Existing scholarship has yet to examine whether
these practices can be understood as a management system with specific functions, processes, and
norms; or whether eco-documentaries can be approached as a body of material or an archive from
which such a system can be reconstructed. The originality of this research lies in the
conceptualization of indigenous women’s agrarian practices as an Indigenous Management
System, rather than only as ecological stewardship or resistance, and in situating the functions of
management within the visual archive of eco-documentaries. The definition of an Indigenous
Management System used throughout this research is a community-based, knowledge-based
system for identifying, assigning, monitoring, conserving and transmitting ecological resources.
First and foremost, the research asks how women in these communities identify, allocate, monitor

and transmit ecological resources and knowledge, and what functions tie these arrangements
together. Second, it asks how an eco-documentary represents such an arrangement in order to make
them visible and understandable to external audiences. It is the eco-documentaries that are
interpreted, not the management system itself; the data consist of the films themselves, not the
practices depicted in them. Common-pool resource scholarship provides the dominant analytical
lens, yet the documentaries provide greater evidence of the operationally-oriented aspects of
management than of the institutionally-structured rules, institutions and sanctions that constitute
typical features of common-pool resource governance. Traditional ecological knowledge further
provides substance to the practices studied; an eco-feminist perspective assists in explaining why
the knowledge and labor associated with resource stewardship are most commonly found among
women. Documentary is selected as a source of information because much of this management
system operates implicitly, known only in practice, and is poorly documented in written or
numerical records. Methodologically, the study utilizes a comparative case-study methodology
applied to two documentaries. The first eco-documentary is Strength in Diversity: Stress-Tolerant
Crops of Nagaland (2018), directed by Khiamniungan, which describes women’s endeavours to
maintain and cultivate stress-tolerant climate resilient crops in Nagaland, and the second is Saving
the Millets: West Garo Hills (2018), directed by Sangma, which portrays a revival in millet
cultivation in Meghalaya and the conservation of seed for future cultivators. Together, the practices
illustrated in these films may be interpreted as decentralized management systems based on
biodiversity-local decision-making-reciprocity-traditional ecological knowledge. Through this
comparison, three broad principles relating to management emerged. First is decentralized
resource governance where decision making occurs at levels closest to where effects occur. Second
is biodiversity-based management which views agro-biodiversity preservation as criteria for
effective management and as basis for long term adaptation capacity rather than short-term yields.
Third is inter-generational knowledge-management. It views ecological knowledge as a resource
that must be actively transferred between generations if the management system is going to
continue. These principles relate closely to ongoing discussions about sustainability, including
visions such as Viksit Bharat 2047, that include conscious, community-based utilization of
resources (Mission LiFE) when they are viewed as management rather than as activism or folklore.
Lastly, they support recognition of indigenous women as knowledge-holders rather than mere
recipients or beneficiaries of programmes devised externally. The study contributes to the

scholarship on indigenous management by offering empirical evidence that seed selection,
biodiversity maintenance and knowledge transmission are all processes of decentralised resource
governance, and by documenting practices that remain largely oral and embodied. Through two
eco-documentaries from the Indo-Burma border, it identifies commons-oriented forms of
indigenous resource governance in India that carry real significance for the country’s path toward a
sustainable and equitable Viksit Bharat.
Keywords: Indigenous Management Systems, Indigenous women, Agrarian commons,
Traditional Ecological Knowledge, Eco-documentaries.
